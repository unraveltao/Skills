# Research guidelines

## When to research

Research when current or external facts can alter the choice: whether a product already exists, the state of an open-source project, licensing, platform limits, security requirements, likely cost, or evidence of user need. Do not browse just to decorate a stable conceptual answer.

## Evidence practice

- Prefer primary sources for product capabilities, project status, licenses, standards, and technical constraints: official product pages, repositories, documentation, release notes, and standards bodies.
- Use independent sources for market or user evidence when primary claims are promotional. Look for counterexamples, limitations, abandoned projects, and negative user experiences.
- Record the source, relevant date, and the specific claim it supports. A source mentioning a project does not establish that it is maintained, suitable, or successful.
- Separate **Fact** (directly supported), **Inference** (reasoned from facts), **Assumption** (not yet verified), and **Recommendation** (value judgment based on stated criteria).
- Do not treat search snippets as sufficient evidence for consequential claims. Open the source when tools allow.
- Disclose unavailable pages, tool limits, stale evidence, and remaining uncertainty. Never invent citations, user research, pricing, or license conclusions.

## Reusable projects and licensing

Identify what can be reused at the level of capability and interface before proposing code reuse. If adapting a specific project, check its current license and relevant conditions from the project or authoritative license text. If you cannot verify those conditions, say so and keep the reuse proposal conditional. Do not copy third-party instructions or code into deliverables without checking permission and attribution requirements.

## Research output

For each material finding, provide a concise claim, evidence and citation, relevance to the decision, and caveat. Conclude with what the evidence supports and what it does not establish. A short task may need only a few linked findings; deep research should preserve enough context for another person to reproduce the decision.
